## Fable — methodology and economics

# Referee report 1 — methodology and economics

## Summary

The contribution replaces a toy election model with one where the electorate (120,408 adult rows, `us_2026_measured.meta.json` counts), the platforms (two encoded 2026 federal tax reforms, `democrasim/engine/reforms.py`), and per-household impacts are engine-computed, leaving perception as the single labeled assumption (`perception.py`). The software is genuinely good: 90 behavioral tests pass (`uv run pytest`), `demo` and the sweeps run as documented, the artifact carries exemplary provenance (certified dataset SHA-256, reform dicts, extraction validation in `engine/build.py:187–277`), aggregation conventions (vote per adult, welfare per household) are implemented correctly, and framing is scrupulously generic and disclaimed. I re-derived every headline number I checked — margin shares (78.7%/21.3%), the $4.11 levy gap, decile incidence (0.099/0.997 from the committed parquet), thresholds 0.5208/0.5075, bias tolerance $219.49, the approval and abstention rows — and all reproduce. My concerns are not about correctness of the computations but about what the headline claims are claims *of*: the perfect-accuracy result's direction is a calibration-residual artifact presented as a finding; the welfare benchmark is applied inconsistently across voting rules; the accuracy axis is not the survey-comparable scale it claims to be; the entry threshold is electorate-size-specific; the note cites no literature for mechanisms that are classical; and several committed results have no committed generator.

## Verdict: major revisions

## Major points

1. **The perfect-accuracy "0% tracked" direction is the sign of a 2.9% calibration residual; state it as welfare-*independence*, not anti-tracking.** The no-stakes bloc votes B solely because B costs $38.0B vs A's $39.1B (`descriptives.json`: levies $142.05 vs $146.16). I reran with B's gross deltas rescaled to cost 3% *more* than A, refinanced per capita: 97% of adults then prefer A and elections track 100% at perfect accuracy (welfare ranking unchanged). So the committed 0% and a hypothetical 100% differ by a coin flip on residual budget mismatch — which the authors chose when "budget-matching within 3%". `findings.md` §3 honestly calls it a knife-edge and shows the parity counterfactual (gap → abstention → 100%), but never states the sharper truth: at perfect accuracy the outcome is decided entirely by the levy-gap *sign*, a quantity orthogonal to the welfare ranking. The README table (rows 44–46) headlines "tracking collapses" without this. Note also the internal tension with `reforms.py:8` ("neither is simply 'the bigger giveaway'"): at high accuracy the election is decided by exactly which is the bigger giveaway. Fix: add the sign-flip counterfactual to §3's table, and reword the headline claim to "perfect accuracy hands the election to a welfare-irrelevant sign".

2. **The welfare benchmark is defensible for plurality but inconsistently applied to approval.** Grading "tracked" as best-of-two is fine for a forced binary choice, and findings.md:20–24 flags that both financed policies score below the status quo (isoelastic: −229,517 vs −514,508; my recomputation matches `descriptives.json`). But §6 then celebrates approval electing Policy A "100% of the time" as the fix. Approval's whole advertised virtue is that it offers an implicit "neither" — and the metric says "neither" *is* the welfare optimum. Graded against the three-option ranking its own metric induces, approval at perfect accuracy fails 100% of the time (elects A, welfare −229,517 < 0). The setup's parenthetical "(the approval result below is the exception)" does not resolve this. The code knows: `election.py:95` yields *negative* regret when the status quo persists. Fix: grade approval against the benchmark that includes the status quo, or reframe §6 as "approval reproduces the abstention filter", dropping "welfare-preferred" language; use "constrained welfare ranking" consistently.

3. **"Mean ranking accuracy" is not the survey-comparable, world-invariant scale it is claimed to be** (README:100–106, `experiments.py:13–17`). Three problems, all verifiable: (a) It is financing-rule-dependent — the same perception technology (σ=$100) scores 0.641 on the financed electorate but 0.991 on the gross one (I computed both via `ranking_accuracy`; the gross world excludes the 75.7% exact-zero-margin adults, `perception.py:206–210`). A survey cannot locate voters on an axis defined over margins that include a $4.11 modeling construct voters have never seen. (b) It is not a sufficient statistic: in the committed `bias_sweep_measured.csv`, accuracy *rises* from 0.582 to 0.817 (bias $0→$2,400) while tracking falls 100%→0%, because bias toward the majority's true preference improves measured "accuracy". The accuracy→tracking map is multivalued; plotting sweeps on this axis invites misreading. (c) The population mean blends a 76% bloc at ~0.5 with a highly accurate minority, so "elections track at 0.52" cannot be read as "voters need be barely better than coin flips" (§4 gestures at this but the axis fights the text). Fix: make σ the primary axis with accuracy as a secondary annotation per world; scope the survey-comparability claim to the perception parameters (attenuation, bias, σ), which genuinely are survey-estimable — not to the accuracy scale.

4. **The entry threshold is specific to n_voters = 10,001 and the note never says so.** The homogeneous threshold 0.5075 is just the jury-theorem quantity 0.5 + z₀.₉·0.5/√n; the measured 0.5208 inherits the same scaling. I verified: at σ=21,544 (accuracy 0.5075), p_tracked is 0.50 with 10,001 voters but 0.70 with 100,001 (60 elections each). At national scale both entry thresholds collapse toward 0.5 and the measured-vs-toy comparison (0.521 vs 0.507) compresses to nothing, while the ~0.67 ceiling (an expected-vote-share crossing) is approximately n-invariant. Fix: report the n-scaling explicitly, add one n-sensitivity panel, and temper the "≈0.52" headline (README:44) to an n-indexed statement.

5. **Zero citations; the mechanisms are classical and the note should position its genuine contribution — dollar-denominated quantification on measured stakes.** The "moderate noise lets the high-stakes minority decide" regime is the probabilistic-voting aggregation result (Lindbeck–Weibull 1987; Coughlin–Nitzan 1981: idiosyncratic noise makes plurality weight stakes, noise→0 reverts to counting heads) — §2 numerically traverses that transition without naming it. Robustness-to-noise vs fragility-to-correlated-bias is the miracle-of-aggregation debate (Page–Shapiro 1992 vs Caplan 2007) and the correlated-errors jury-theorem breakdown (Ladha 1992), named only as "the jury theorem dies with correlated errors" (§5). The trivial-stakes majority outvoting an intense minority is Dahl's (1956) intensity problem; the $25 abstention band is Downsian; abstention-by-the-uninformed rescuing the outcome is kin to Feddersen–Pesendorfer's (1996) swing voter's curse; misperceived tax incidence has a measurement literature (Bartels 2005; Stantcheva 2021) directly relevant to issue #3. Claiming no novelty is honest but insufficient — without positioning, the note implicitly presents known results as discoveries. A ~half-page related-work section fixes this and *strengthens* the contribution claim: nobody has put dollar magnitudes ($219 bias vs $1,000 noise; a $4.11 constructed margin) on these mechanisms using measured microdata.

6. **Several committed results have no committed generator, and one findings row has no committed result.** `descriptives.json`, `bias_sweep_fine.csv`, `bias_tolerance_fine.json`, and `robustness_abstain25.json` are tracked but produced by no script in the repo (`scripts/robustness.py` and `cli.py sweep` generate neither; I grepped). The abstain-$10 row (findings.md:108) and the "deficit-financed perception behaves like the gross row" claim (findings.md:123–125) have no committed evidence at all — I verified both by rerunning (0%/53% and the $25 case 100%/24% reproduce), so this is a traceability defect, not an accuracy one. The decile shares (9.9%/99.7%, findings.md:33–34) come from `build.py report()` against gitignored intermediates, though they are derivable from the committed parquet (I reproduced 0.099/0.997). Fix: extend `scripts/robustness.py` (or add `scripts/descriptives.py`) to emit every committed results file, and list it in findings' Reproduction section.

## Minor points

- `p_tracked_se = √(p(1−p)/240)` reports 0 at boundaries (`experiments.py:53`); "Policy B wins every election" at n=240 means ≤1.25% at 95% (rule of three). Use Wilson intervals.
- `headline.json` carries `tolerance_dollars: 200.0` — a pure grid artifact of interpolating one 0→1 step on the coarse $400 grid — while the text quotes the fine-grid 219.49. Two committed, conflicting numbers; drop or label the coarse one. Also, ±$25-step interpolation between p=0.21 and p=0.95 doesn't support three significant figures; "≈$220" with grid width stated would be honest.
- Utilitarian-on-financed welfare ranks B first by float noise (−4.05e−6 vs −4.24e−6, `descriptives.json`); `welfare_optimal` (`welfare.py:35–37`) has no near-tie guard. Declare degenerate rankings rather than argmax-ing noise.
- "Voters rationally abstain over sub-$25 annual stakes" (findings.md:112): pivotality calculus doesn't rationalize a $25 cutoff — at n≈10⁴ pivot probabilities make even $1,000 stakes borderline; at national scale all stakes are sub-threshold. Call the band behavioral.
- Isoelastic welfare uses unequivalized household income with a flat $1,000 floor despite `household_size` being in the artifact. I checked per-capita and √-scale equivalization: A≻B and A-below-status-quo both survive, so a documented caveat suffices — but the omission should be stated in "What would move these results".
- Perception noise is drawn independently per adult (`perception.py:82–89`), so spouses seeing one household delta get independent errors; household-correlated errors would enlarge the effective levy-bloc signal. Worth one sentence.
- The "tracking ≈100% for σ ∈ [$50, $2,000]" band understates its own CSV (0.992 at σ=4,642), and the collapse edge (accuracy 0.667→0.720 spans p=1.0→0.067) sits between two grid points; the 13-point geomspace under-resolves the two most-quoted features. One RNG consumed sequentially across grid points is statistically fine but couples the whole grid to any grid edit; spawn per-point substreams.
- README uses 76% (zero-both), 79%/97% (per-policy), and "roughly three-quarters" for adjacent quantities; define each once.

## Questions for the authors

1. The certified dataset is a *sparse* build (`buildj-sparse-rmloss100`, meta.json:51). Were the inputs these two reforms depend on verified unaffected by sparsification, and do $39.1B/$38.0B benchmark against any external revenue score?
2. Why is per-capita levy the headline financing rule, given the knife-edge lives entirely inside the financing construction and "proportional" merely replicates it? Is there a principled default?
3. For approval (major point 2): is the intended benchmark two-option or three-option, and if the latter, what does §6 claim?
4. Does 10,001 voters represent anything (a jurisdiction? computational budget)? Would you commit an n-scaling result?
5. Bias enters as a constant dollar shift per policy. Have you examined stake-proportional or group-correlated bias (via `GroupedPerception`), and does "$219/voter/yr" survive as the right summary statistic?

No committed number is affected (the rules CSVs use financed data, where no margin is exactly zero — I checked), but findings.md line 168-169's "with two policies, instant-runoff is arithmetically identical to plurality" is false in general under this implementation; it holds on the financed electorate only because indifference has measure zero there. Give IRV an explicit indifference/abstention policy or scope the claim to "absent exact indifference."

4. **Degenerate welfare grading is silent.** `Utilitarian().per_policy` on the financed electorate returns [−4.24e-06, −4.05e-06] — float dust — and `welfare_optimal` (welfare.py:35-37) confidently returns Policy B; `run_election(welfare=Utilitarian())` on financed data then grades `tracked`/`regret` against summation noise. findings.md line 194 knows this ("η=0 is degenerate under exact budget neutrality") but the API doesn't: any user composing the README quickstart with `Utilitarian()` gets an arbitrary benchmark with no warning. Related guard inconsistency: `analytic_ranking_accuracy` raises on n_policies ≠ 2 (perception.py:98-99), but the Monte Carlo fallback (perception.py:196-204) silently scores only the first two policies of a 3-policy electorate — I confirmed it returns 1.0 without error. Suggest: raise or warn in `welfare_optimal`/`run_election` when the welfare spread is below float-noise scale relative to Σ|w·δ/a|, and add the two-policy check to the MC path.

## Sol — methodology and economics

# Referee report 1 — methodology and economics (Sol)

## Summary

This is a transparent, useful research-software rebuild: the encoded reforms and committed artifact have unusually good provenance, the generic Policy A/B framing is neutral, and I reproduced the core and scripted robustness outputs byte-for-byte. `uv run pytest` returned 90 passes; `uv run democrasim demo` and a full sweep to `/tmp` succeeded. The central interpretation is not yet valid, however. The reported 0.52 threshold and “Gaussian toy never tracks” result depend materially—and in the latter case qualitatively—on an unexplained 10,001-voter resample rather than the represented 268M-adult polity. The non-monotonicity is a deliberately disclosed but still over-headlined discontinuity generated by a 2.9% residual cost mismatch, post-engine financing, forced choice, and participation. The welfare benchmark is ballot-restricted even though both policies lose to current law, and the documented welfare sensitivity contains a false claim. I recommend major revisions, not rejection: the contribution can become a strong calibrated counterexample and reusable simulation framework.

## Verdict: major revisions

## Major points

1. **Effective electorate size drives two headline conclusions.** Evidence: every election PPS-samples 10,001 adults with replacement (`democrasim/electorate.py:144-154`; `democrasim/election.py:35-44`; `democrasim/cli.py:250-252`), although the artifact represents 267,550,525 adults (`democrasim/data/us_2026_measured.meta.json:86-90`). Under the implemented iid two-option model, winner probabilities are exact binomial tails. My calculation from the parquet put the lower 90%-tracking accuracy at 0.5923 for N=101, 0.5381 for 1,001, 0.5190 for 10,001, and about 0.5017 for 268M. More seriously, the target Gaussian margin has mean $6.81 and SD $4,007, hence perfect-information A support 0.500678: approximately a coin flip at 10,001, but virtually certain A victory at 268M. Thus “never tracks” (`docs/findings.md:61-78`) is not a distributional result. Fix: justify the polity, report N-sensitivity and the large-population limit, use exact support/binomial calculations for binary plurality, and distinguish repeated perception draws in a fixed electorate from repeatedly resampling its composition. Preserve households if modeling correlated co-resident voters.

2. **The non-monotonicity has the epistemic status of a financing counterexample, not a measured-election finding.** Evidence: A distributes $39.1043B and B $38.0062B (`democrasim/data/us_2026_measured.meta.json:16-17,31-32`); `apply_financing` charges each policy’s own cost through an assumed poll tax (`democrasim/welfare.py:91-122`). The $1.098B residual mechanically makes every gross-unaffected positive-income household prefer cheaper B. Exact parity restores 100% tracking at perfect information (`docs/results/robustness_summary.json:20-24`), and reversing the tiny cost ordering reverses that bloc; proportional financing preserves the same cheaper-policy sign. This matters because the decisive financed packages are not wholly “actual encoded reforms,” and “everything except perception is measured” (`README.md:6-23`) is false: financing, welfare, turnout, ballot, electorate size, and error dependence are assumptions. Fix: present the result as an existence/diagnostic case; make exact parity or an encoded realistic financing package primary; add signed cost-gap and progressive-financing sensitivities; and say “gross impacts are model-computed; financing and behavioral/normative layers are modeled.”

3. **The welfare estimand and implementation need correction and sensitivity analysis.** Evidence: “optimal” is only `argmax` over the two policy columns (`democrasim/welfare.py:35-37`), while both log-welfare changes are negative, about −229,517 and −514,508 (`docs/results/descriptives.json:31-34`). Approval permits individual non-approval but still selects only A or B (`democrasim/voting.py:97-105`), so it is not a collective status-quo option. The SWF applies utility to total household income with one weight per household and no equivalence scale (`welfare.py:80-88`), consequential for a child credit. It also floors baseline before adding the delta. My direct run found `Isoelastic(eta=0,floor=1000)` gives $82.5M/$80.1M rather than utilitarian zero, contradicting `welfare.py:53-54`; at eta=3 and the stated $1,000 floor, B ranks above A, contradicting “higher eta strengthens A” (`docs/findings.md:192-196`). Fix: call the estimand “ballot-restricted/pairwise welfare preference,” add current law as a real alternative or approval quorum, special-case/correct eta=0, define co-optimality/tie tolerance, and report floor × eta × household/adult/member/equivalence-scale sensitivities.

4. **Mean ranking accuracy is a useful coordinate, not a sufficient or universally survey-comparable scale; statistical precision is overstated.** Evidence: it averages correct-sign probabilities while excluding zero margins (`democrasim/perception.py:174-210`). In the bias run, mean accuracy rises from 0.582 to 0.635 while tracking falls from 1 to 0 (`docs/results/bias_sweep_fine.csv:2-8`); gross/parity exclude roughly 76% of adults from the denominator. The model also assumes homoskedastic errors independent across both policy assessments (`perception.py:82-97`). Meanwhile 240 draws use a Wald SE that becomes zero at 0/240 or 240/240 (`democrasim/experiments.py:45-59`), and `find_threshold` linearly interpolates the first crossing of a non-monotone noisy curve (`experiments.py:155-179`). Conditional on N=10,001, my exact check gives the two 90% boundaries at accuracy 0.5190 and 0.7054, not merely a lower “threshold” and the claimed ≈0.67 ceiling; the exact median bias root is $220.30, consistent with the prose’s ≈$219 but showing the interpolation need not be simulated. Fix: report a tracking band, exact probabilities where available, Wilson/Jeffreys intervals otherwise, and accuracy by preference side/stake bin plus tie mass and residual covariance. A single sequential RNG is unbiased but grid-order-dependent; use named substreams or documented common random numbers.

5. **The theoretical positioning and comparator claims need tightening.** The measured setting is not the classic Condorcet common-value problem: voters have conflicting private stakes and “correct” is an external SWF. Heterogeneous competence under independent errors is within generalized jury-theorem logic; common directional bias defeating aggregation is known. Gaussian perception is a probit/random-utility device familiar from probabilistic voting. Likewise, a hard $25 annual-stake cutoff (`docs/findings.md:111-120`) is not “rational abstention”: costly voting depends on voting costs and pivotal probability, while rational ignorance concerns information acquisition. Add a compact related-work section covering these literatures and state the novelty as quantitative incidence calibration/software. The Gaussian toy matches adult-weighted Pearson moments, then sets uniform weights and one-adult households (`democrasim/toy.py:29-60`), whereas welfare uses household weights; moment matching neither preserves rank correlation nor guarantees nonlinear welfare. Repeat toy seeds/preserve household structure. Call the homogeneous toy a canonical CJT benchmark, not the old model, whose archived voter code actually drew heterogeneous weights, accuracies, bias, and turnout (`git show 221b30e:democrasim/core/voter.py`, lines 22-47, 73-98, 116-165).

6. **Some claims lack a committed generating path, and one robustness sentence is false.** The full sweep reproduced its eight result files exactly, and redirected `scripts/robustness.py` reproduced its five files.
